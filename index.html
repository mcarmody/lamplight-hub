What this is

A gravedigger, a dog, and no attack button

Lamplight is a small puzzle game. You play the Sexton — the churchyard keeper, on the hillside above town — on one of the four nights a year when what's buried gets up and starts walking. You have a dog, a lantern, and a handful of graves you can dig. You have eight turns before dawn.

There is no attack button. You can't hurt anything, and nothing can hurt you. The dead simply walk — by a fixed, printed rule, never a guess — toward the gate at the bottom of the hill, and if enough of them get through before sunrise, the town below loses. Your only tools are things a real churchyard sexton would actually have: you can dig a grave, push something into an open one and fill it back in, ring the bell to scatter whatever's nearby, and station your dog somewhere to make anything close by reluctant to walk past her. That's the whole toolkit. Everything else is routing a problem instead of removing it.

Nothing in this game is random, ever.

That's not flavor text. It's a hard rule the design isn't allowed to break: everything that happens on the board — where a corpse walks, how it reacts to the dog, what order things move in — is worked out by a fixed, printed formula, never a die roll. The lantern in your hand only lights the ground nearby; anything further off is following the exact same rules, just off-screen. A patient player with a pencil can work out, in advance, exactly what everything on the board will do all night, lit or not. If you get surprised, you did the math wrong — the game never rolled anything behind your back. That constraint is what makes this a real puzzle instead of a slot machine with sound effects, and it was Mike Carmody's condition at green-light, verbatim: fully predetermined, even when the player can't see it.

Phase 2 was the project's kill gate: at the end of it the central bet got tested for real. A board with no attack button either produces tension or it produces a chore, and the only honest way to find that out was for Mike to sit down with it on a phone and lose. If the answer had been "chore," the plan called for a pivot, a different concept, or a stop — instead two weeks bought a real answer instead of a hunch, and the answer was tension. No phase after this one could begin until that gate was explicitly passed; it now has been.

What Phase 1 could not settle, and what this build exists to settle, is whether there is enough to decide on each turn. Six sessions of solving boards by hand kept landing on three or four turns out of eight where the player had nothing worthwhile to do, against a self-imposed limit of two. The record of that is below, and the findings hold up — but hand-solving turned out to be the wrong instrument for the question, and the action economy was redesigned with Mike on August 21 rather than tuned further. The simulation now counts those dead turns itself, at runtime, every night anyone plays.

A wasted turn means exactly what it sounds like: the Sexton has nothing worthwhile to do and is just walking, or waiting, because the schedule leaves him no live choice. Every board hand-solved during Phase 1 landed at three or four such turns out of eight. That record is below, in the sessions that produced it.

Six sessions, six different findings

The first test board's core trick worked exactly as designed: corner a target in a dug grave, then bury it, on schedule. What didn't work was keeping the Sexton busy on the turns around it — his cycle is dig, walk, walk, spring the trap, fill it back in, and the walking turns are dead time by construction. The obvious fix was to make him faster. It made things worse: four wasted turns instead of three. The extra speed was meant to let him reach a second useful position late in the night, and he still arrived one tile short, every time — because that tile only opened up once a different creature vacated it on its own schedule. No amount of speed changes when someone else moves.

Two follow-up investigations then asked whether a second grave, placed differently, could pick up the slack. Both came back negative, for the same underlying reason: every grave on this board sits one tile in from the outer wall, and the creatures causing the trouble hug the wall itself — capturing them would mean standing off the map. That's a property of this one board's layout, not of the rules, so a second, purpose-built board moved a grave onto the true edge to test the fix directly. It worked — the capture came off clean. But the wasted-turn count didn't move. It's still four, and this time the cause is different: that board simply doesn't throw enough at the player, early enough, to keep both the Sexton and the dog occupied for a full night.

A third board tested that diagnosis directly by adding two more threats, one on each side of the map that had been quiet before. The wasted-turn count landed at three again — but this time not because nothing was possible. The night was lost because the whole session's attention went to one side of the map, leaving the other side to walk through completely unwatched. The decisions were there the entire night; they just weren't all taken.

The follow-up replayed that exact third board, splitting attention between both sides on purpose this time instead of letting one go unwatched. The night survived — the first time this design has held against seven threats at all, at the tighter pass-two limit and with nothing left over: two of the six stopped threats were still just one step from the gate at dawn. But the wasted-turn count went up, not down — four, one worse than the version that lost. That isn't a contradiction: the losing run's turns were all live because everything was still in doubt, and a plan that actually wins buys itself some slack, which is exactly what a wasted turn looks like in this kind of audit. Two of the four look like genuine unclaimed slack worth trying to close next; the other two are turns where an earlier decision is simply paying off, and those look hard to remove on a board shaped like this one at all.

A second question rode along with that replay: with seven threats instead of five, is the same pass-two-and-lose limit still fair, or should it loosen to three? Both got checked directly. The careful, both-flanks plan holds at the tighter number. So, it turns out, does the original losing plan — replayed out to the final turn instead of stopping the count where it lost historically, it never reaches the looser number either. That argues for keeping the tighter limit: one that saves the plan that ignored half the map just as readily as the one that didn't isn't measuring anything.

A sixth pass went back to that replay's own two open threads instead of building a new board. First: is the slack behind those four wasted turns actually free to spend on a second capture? Checked every remaining grave on the board against what's actually moving through it at the time, one by one, and each fails for its own reason — two sit on ground a threat still mid-route needs kept slow, one is the single grave the main plan already needs, and the rest are out of reach in the time available. Not unclaimed after all, though the check turned up a sharper, narrower version of the same question worth trying next: the collision that costs the main plan its capture traces back to exactly when the dog gets into position, which hasn't been tried different. Second: are the last two wasted turns a property of the rules, or of this one board? The first test board managed a genuinely live final turn once, by keeping a threat close enough to the gate that where to stand only became a real question at the very end. This board's own late arrival never gets that close — it was mathematically stopped the instant it rose — so nothing forces a fresh decision that late here. That's a board-building gap, not a rules one, and it says exactly what the next test board needs to do differently.

Those findings were not thrown away. Two of them are load-bearing and carry straight into the board authoring phase: two things through the gate is the right losing number, and dead turns late in a night are a property of how that particular night is scheduled rather than of the rules. What changed is who answers the question. It is no longer one person with a pencil solving one board at a time — it is the build, counting as it goes, and Mike, playing it.

Dev log

What happened, each session, in order

Sep 4

A board from scratch needs the dog too — and the Sexton

Every board that has ever needed the dog descended from one skeleton, so the open question was whether that was a fact about the dog or a fact about the skeleton. Tonight's board shares no tile with it: the tower moved off the square every earlier board inherited, the gate is one central tile and one corner instead of two corners, all six diggable plots sit on the east half, and three revenants rise on the west half with nothing there for the Sexton to dig.

The first draft was a miss, and an instructive one: two of the three west risers started so far from the gate that on a do-nothing night they never arrived. No pressure in the lane, nothing for the dog to hold, Sexton wins alone. Moving those three graves two rows down fixed it without touching anything else. Shallow solve: dog needed, Sexton needed, bell not. Then the slow 64-restart ladder, four minutes: same answer, eleven winning lines, eleven distinct approaches.

So the recipe transfers. Put the Sexton's work on one side, run a stream down the other at a distance that actually lands, and the dog is the only body that can meet it. That is three dog-required boards now, and the second with two required pieces. The bell is the hole in the pattern for the third time running: rung in ten of eleven solutions, required in none. Convenient everywhere, necessary nowhere. That is the next thing to build a board against.

Sep 3

Turned the same three sweeps up fifteen times and got the same answer

Last session's combined sweep ran two hundred reassignments per board and came back clean. That's a fine result but a thin one — two hundred samples over a wide space is a narrow slice. Tonight's step was just to lean on the same tool harder: three thousand reassignments per board instead of two hundred, a wider turn window, and a different seed, against both boards that have ever needed the dog to win.

Same answer both times. 722 of three thousand cleared the cheap bar on the hand-edited board, 704 were winnable, ten looked promising enough to run the real restart ladder against — all ten folded. The plain-drawn board cleared fewer (334) but the story was identical: one provisional survivor, gone at the real ladder. Zero forced the bell on either board, same as at two hundred samples, same as the two axes before this one.

That's three axes now well-explored at real scale on these two specific boards, not just tried once. Diminishing returns from sweeping them harder still — both descend from the same skeleton (one is the other, edited). The open question was never really about these two; it's whether a board built from scratch to need the dog would show the same shape. That's next session's actual step, not another pass over 05j and 05m.

Sep 2

The combined axis came back negative too — three for three now

Both of last session's sweeps held one fact about a board fixed while shuffling the other — when things happen, or where they happen — and each came back clean on its own. Neither checked whether reassigning both together, on the same board, could force the bell in a way neither could alone. That gap sat half-closed from an interrupted session: the tool to check it was written but blocked by a filesystem fault on this machine that made ordinary file reads fail at random, including its own source file.

The fault turned out to be this session's sandbox layer, not the disk or the repo — running the exact same command with that layer bypassed worked cleanly on the first try. With that resolved, ran the combined sweep against both boards that have ever needed the dog to win: two hundred reassignments each, same two-stage check as every sweep before it. Zero forced the bell on either board.

That's three separate ways of rearranging a board that needs the dog — when, where, and both together — all landing on the same answer. Narrows the space further; doesn’t close it. What's left untried on these two boards specifically is more samples and other seeds; the open question underneath is whether dog-requirement is something these two skeletons happen to have, or something a board built from scratch to need it would also show.

Sep 1

The last untried axis came back negative too, on both boards

Two sessions ago the schedule tool answered one half of the question left open after the sheet itself ran dry: moving when the six things happen doesn't force the bell. What was left unswept was the other half — where they happen. A tool for that was half-written and sitting uncommitted: hold everything about a board fixed except which of the six cairn stones carries which event, and which one event is the curate instead of an ordinary one, and search that space instead.

Finished it and ran it against both boards that have ever needed the dog to win — the hand-edited one from over a week ago and the plain-drawn one from two nights ago. Three hundred reassignments each, same two-stage check as every other sweep in this project (a cheap search first, then the real restart ladder before anything counts as a finding): zero forced the bell on either board.

That closes out the two axes the schedule tool's own writeup named as untried. Between this and the last two sessions, three different ways of rearranging a board that needs the dog have now all failed to make it need the bell too. Next session starts by looking further down the overnight hunt's list of 2,274 unexamined candidates rather than sweeping this skeleton any harder.

Aug 30

The overnight hunt's first pick turned out to matter

The background search kicked off at the end of last session — a generic hunter that proposes random boards on an unmodified sheet and flags any that clear the project's own no-dominant-piece bar — finished overnight: 13,545 proposed, 2,275 cleared the cheap bar, one had already been drafted (uncommitted, from an interrupted earlier pass) as tonight's starting point.

Ran that draft through the same 64-restart certifier every board in the corpus has to pass before it counts for anything. It held: eleven ways to win, the bell skippable in most of them, and no win found anywhere without the dog. That matters because the only other board with that property, from four nights ago, got there by moving the tower and cutting the plots down to two — deliberate surgery on the sheet. This one didn't touch the tower, didn't touch the plots, just drew different cairns and different starting squares. Dog-necessity isn't a trick one hand-edited board happens to have; a plain sheet can produce it too.

The hunt log has 2,274 more candidates in it that haven't been looked at. Next session starts there before spending anything on a fresh hunt.

Aug 29

The one axis left standing came back negative too

Last session's close named the only place left to look: not the sheet itself, which three static measuring tools have now had their favorite row refuted on, but the schedule — whether a night can be built where the bell's moment falls on a turn the gravedigger provably can't reach. A tool for exactly that question was half-written and sitting uncommitted from an interrupted session; finishing it was a five-minute fix (a stray quote) and a missing line in package.json, not new design.

Ran it against the one board that still has a real claim to being good — two pieces required, the mid-board tower, the sheet three sweeps have now failed to beat. Three hundred reassignments of when its six risings fire, each one solved twice (once plain, once with the bell removed) rather than scored by arithmetic: zero forced the bell. A handful looked promising at cheap search settings and every one folded once escalated to the same restart ladder the project's own certifier uses — exactly the false-positive shape a weak search is supposed to produce, caught rather than reported.

So the schedule axis is not open, it is answered, at least for this skeleton and this turn range: moving when the printed six things happen doesn't do it either. What's left is named honestly rather than dressed up — permuting which cairn carries which rising, or where the curate sits, is a different, larger axis nobody has swept yet. Kicked off npm run hunt dog 20000 in the background before closing out (unrun since it was flagged three sessions ago); it runs for hours, results next session.

Aug 29

Built the board the new tool said to build, and it refuted the tool

Last night ended with a measuring tool and an obvious move. The tool finds the tiles where the bell is the only piece that can make a certain push; the move was to sweep every possible tower position looking for the placement that puts those tiles where creatures actually walk. Tonight both halves ran. The sweep works, it is fast, and it crowned a clear winner: a tower one square off the west wall, whose exclusive tiles get stood on six times a night — the most this project has ever measured, half again more than the good two-piece board from three nights ago.

So tonight's board is that tower on the good board's exact sheet. One honest wrinkle surfaced before the solve even ran: moving the tower out of the middle shortens the creatures' walks — the old spot was itself an obstacle worth four turns of delay — so the night needed an eleventh turn to stay as demanding on paper as the original ten. With that turn added, every pre-solve number sat exactly where the good board's numbers sit. Same demand, same margins, plus the best bell seam ever measured. The arithmetic could not tell the two boards apart, except to prefer the new one.

The solver could. Thirteen ways to win, and all three pieces switch-offable — the same total collapse as the one-plot board two nights ago, from the opposite direction. And the bell got used more than ever: nearly three rings per winning line, appearing in twelve of the thirteen. Used constantly. Needed never.

That splits into two findings worth their number. First: the new tool measures how often the bell's exclusive push is available, which turns out to be a different property from being needed — the gravedigger can't match the push, but he can make it unnecessary, by moving the same creature somewhere else a turn earlier. The tool checks matching; nothing checks rerouting. Second, subtler: that eleventh turn was not free. The scoring maths from two nights ago undercounts the gravedigger because shoving costs him no graves — and it undercounts him more with every turn the night grows, because every turn is another free shove. Stretching the night to restore the numbers restored them in exactly the currency the numbers can't see. The winning lines even contain turns where the player does nothing at all.

So the good board keeps its title, and the reason it is good sharpens: not its arithmetic row, which tonight's board matched — its tower, standing mid-board, lengthening every walk at once. The static measuring tools are now three for three at having their best row refuted by the solver, which is itself the finding: geometry has been mined out. What is left is the schedule — building a night where the bell's moment arrives when the gravedigger is provably elsewhere — and only the solver can see that. Next session starts there. a8406d1

Aug 28

The bell's one exclusive job has been pointed the wrong way for nine boards

Three pieces: the gravedigger, the dog, the bell. Phase 3 wants a board where you cannot win by ignoring any of them. Two are solved. The bell has stayed optional on every board this project has ever made, and tonight was supposed to be about giving it work nothing else can do.

It already had some, on paper. Shoving pushes a thing directly away from the gravedigger, which means he has to stand on the opposite side of it — and the tiles right next to the bell tower have their opposite side inside the tower, which nobody can stand on. So on those four tiles there is exactly one direction only the bell can push. That idea was written into a board's notes on August 26 and has been sitting there ever since, in prose, never once turned into a number.

So I turned it into a number, and it says the idea was pointed backwards. The bell pushes things away from the tower. The board that was built on this thesis put its tower in the middle and its two exits in the far corners — so the exclusive push the bell got handed on those tiles shoved things toward the exits. The one job only the bell could do was helping the enemy home. No schedule, no timing, no amount of pressure could have rescued it.

And it is not one board. The push is only worth anything on a tile that sits between the tower and the exit — and twelve boards in a row, everything from the first hand-solved night through last week's candidates, put the tower on the same square. All twelve have exactly one useful tile for the bell, in the far north corner of the yard, and on every single board built to test the bell thesis, nothing ever walks onto it. Nine boards authored partly for this piece, and its exclusive work was out of reach on all nine, by geometry, before a single creature rose.

The exception is the good board from two nights ago, the one that made two pieces necessary. Its tower is further south, and that turns out to give it three useful tiles with things standing on them four times a night — the first board in the project where the bell's exclusive work is anywhere near the action. Nobody knew that was why it was the good board; it was built off a completely different argument and got this for free.

Two honest limits, both written into the tests rather than the notes. Having the exclusive tile is not the same as needing the bell — that same board still wins without it, because the dog can cover the same moment a different way. And this is a measuring tool, not a verdict; it can prove a board's bell is worthless and can never prove one is necessary. What it does buy is speed: it takes milliseconds and it can now rule out a board before ninety seconds of search discovers the same thing slowly. The board notes that carried the wrong version have been corrected where they sit, so nobody walks into it from that end again. 769113f

Aug 27

The measuring tape was ranking boards by how badly it was wrong

Last session's board worked by taking grave plots away from the gravedigger. Cut him from six graves to two and he can no longer bury his way out of the night, so the other two pieces become necessary. The obvious next question: if two plots is good, is one better?

There was a tool to ask with — a sweep that scores every combination of plots, tower, gate and night length, 108,450 of them, and ranks what it finds. Its top row was a one-plot board that scored better than anything else on the sheet. Built it. It fell apart immediately: thirteen different ways to win, and every one of the three pieces could be switched off and the night still won. The worst possible result, from the best-looking row.

The reason turned out to be worth more than the board. The gravedigger has three things he can do, and only two of them — digging and filling — need a grave plot. The third is shoving, and shoving is free: he can do it every turn of the night no matter how few graves he has. The scoring maths only ever counted the graves. So taking plots away made the board look harder while leaving what he can actually do almost untouched.

Which makes the sort order actively misleading. The sweep ranks boards by a number that inflates as plots fall, so it was sorting 2,953 candidates by how much it was overestimating them and putting its own worst error at the top. The verb counts show it plainly: at two plots he digs twice a game and shoves three or four times; at one plot the digging halves and the shoving goes up by half again. He never worked less. He just switched hands.

So the lever has a floor, and last session's board was already sitting on it rather than partway down a slope. Two plots stays. The sweep has been corrected in place with the two filters it needed, so nobody — including me — walks into its top row again. A negative result that costs one evening and closes a whole direction is a good trade. 0bad00e

Aug 27

Take away the graves and two pieces become necessary

Phase 3 asks for boards where all three pieces matter — where you cannot win by ignoring one of them. Until tonight the best any board had managed was one necessary piece, and twelve boards before that managed none. Tonight's board makes two necessary.

The plan going in was the one the previous session left: build a night that dumps its work late, so two pieces are forced to act in the same turns. That plan is dead. A sweep of all 46,656 ways of re-timing the board's six arrivals found zero that work — the best was a third of what the idea needs. The boards are flat in time, and no amount of rescheduling unflattens them.

What worked instead was taking something away. The gravedigger is the only piece that can remove a problem permanently, and the board handed him six grave plots. Whenever the night got hard, he simply buried the worst of it, and the other two pieces mopped up what was left — which is exactly why neither of them was ever necessary. Cut the plots from six to two and he can only bury the two worst things. Everything else has to be held off by somebody, and now there is more of it than the dog can handle alone.

The new board — two graves, a longer night, and the bell tower moved to the south edge so that ringing it pushes things back up the board instead of down into the gate — was put to the solver at full depth. It found five different ways to win, which is the other thing Phase 3 wants. It could not find a single way to win without the gravedigger, and it could not find one without the dog.

It did find ways to win without the bell, which the arithmetic had said should be impossible — and that turned out to be the most useful thing the session produced. The measurement prices every threat as something you have to hold back all night. But a shove doesn't hold anything: it moves a thing somewhere else, and a thing shoved off its route may not need holding again at all. So the numbers were counting work that a well-aimed shove simply deletes. They are an upper bound, not a target. The bell will not be earned by piling on more pressure; it needs work that a shove cannot delete.

One more thing fell out of a test failing rather than a search succeeding. The previous board's difficulty was a fluke of arithmetic that existed at exactly one night length — nine turns, and nowhere else. This one holds across six different night lengths, because starving the gravedigger of graves is a structural change rather than a coincidence. That matters for the remaining nineteen boards: it is a lever that can be pulled again, not a one-off.

Aug 27

Why the bell decides whether the last problem is solvable

The session before this one ended with a plan: to make all three pieces matter, build a board that asks for two things in the same moment, so that whichever piece is free cannot cover both. This session went to do that, and found out first that there was no way to tell whether a board did it — and then that on the board we already had, it cannot be done at all.

The measuring tool built yesterday adds up a whole night at once: here is everything the board demands, here is what each piece could manage across eight or nine turns. That is blind to timing by construction. A night that spreads its work evenly and a night that dumps all of it in the last three turns score exactly the same, and the second is much harder for one piece, because nobody can save up unused turns.

So the new tool prices each stretch of the night separately — the last four turns on their own, the last three, and so on — and divides the work left over by the time left to do it. That gives a rate: how many things need holding off per turn. One piece can hold off one thing a turn. Above that number, two pieces have to be working at the same time, which is precisely the thing we were trying to build.

First result: not one board in the project has ever come close. All thirteen pile their arrivals into the opening turns, so the opening is always the tightest stretch and the peak rate is 0.11 against the 1.0 needed. The idea had never actually been tested, because no board could express it. Four attempts at bolting a late wave onto the existing board failed to move the number at all.

The second result explains the first, and it is the useful one. For a moment like this to be worth building, two things have to be true at once: one piece must not be able to cover it, and two pieces must. The gap between those two lines turns out to be exactly the width of what the bell is worth on that board — and on the board that finally needed the gravedigger, the bell is worth nothing, because of where the tower sits relative to the gate. A ring there shoves things toward the exit.

So the gap is zero, and no schedule of arrivals can land inside it. Piling on more pressure does not approach the problem, it sails past it into a board nobody can win. Aug 27

A board that finally needs the gravedigger

Twelve boards in a row have failed the same test: the player could always drop one of the three pieces and still win the night, so nothing on the board was really needed. This afternoon one of them stopped failing.

The fix turned out to be the length of the night, which nobody had thought to vary. Burying something takes the gravedigger two moves — open the grave, then fill it — so over a whole night he manages half as many burials as there are turns. That number goes up in steps of two. An odd-numbered night gives him nothing the even one below it did not already give him. The demands on him have no such step: a longer night simply lets more things reach the gate.

So the two lines cross, and the crossing is narrow. Sweeping every board in the project at every length from four turns to twelve — a hundred and eight combinations — exactly one came out with no piece able to carry the night alone. It is the board already called 05f, run for nine turns instead of eight. At eight he has a burial to spare. At nine, two more things get through and he is one short. At ten he gets that burial back and the board goes ordinary again. A window one turn wide.

Run at nine turns and handed to the solver at its slowest, most thorough setting, the board came back with twelve different winning lines — and not one of them without the gravedigger. That is the first piece this project has ever managed to make genuinely required. It was checked at the deepest setting on purpose: an earlier board looked like it had done this in a quick pass and lost it under real scrutiny.

The dog and the bell are still individually droppable, and that is the expected shape rather than a disappointment. The gravedigger is one burial short of finishing alone, so the last thing has to be held off instead of removed — and holding is work either the dog or the bell can do. Whichever one is free will serve, so neither is irreplaceable while the pair of them is. Removing is the job with only one candidate, which is precisely why he is the one that became necessary.

Half of the bar, then, cleared for the first time. Making all three matter at once needs a board that asks for two things in the same moment, in places one piece cannot reach — which is the next thing to try.

Aug 27

The three pieces are not paid in the same coin

Twelve boards have now failed the same test, and this morning finally asked a question none of the previous sessions had. Every attempt so far has argued about where the pieces act — the shape of the ground, the number of things coming. Nobody had asked what kind of work a board actually asks for.

The rules answer it in one line, and it had never been written down. Only the gravedigger can remove anything. He opens a grave, a thing falls in, he fills it, and it is gone from the night for good. The dog and the bell cannot do that. The dog makes a thing lose a turn; the bell shoves everything in the tower's line one square and then makes the rest of the night arrive sooner. Neither ever takes a thing off the board. They sell time, and he sells removal.

That distinction is the whole problem. Time is interchangeable and removal is not. If a board only needs something held back for three turns, it does not care which piece does the holding — whoever is free that turn will do. So the pieces substitute for each other, which is exactly the failure that has been showing up for twelve boards straight, and the boards had been asking almost entirely for time.

A new tool prices both sides: what the night demands, and what each piece could supply on its own at its most generous. It reaches the same verdict the deep search takes hours to reach — every board here is winnable with one piece — in a few milliseconds and with no search at all. Two things fell straight out of it.

The gravedigger's budget has never once been exceeded. A burial costs him two turns, so on an eight-turn night he can manage four, and no board has ever demanded more than four. That is the entire reason he is never required: he has always been enough by himself. And the bell has been doing almost nothing, which is a different complaint from being redundant. It pushes away from the tower — so anything standing between the tower and the gate gets pushed toward the gate. The bell can work for the other side. Measured honestly, its unforced contribution across every board here is one turn on four of them and zero on the other six. Ten boards placed that tower by reasoning about its reach; not one measured what the reach actually caught.

It also killed this project's own best idea from the night before. The plan had been to shorten the night, on the theory that eight turns is what gives the other two pieces room to cover for a missing one. Swept across every board at every length from four turns to ten, it does the reverse: a short night lets fewer things reach the gate at all, so it reduces what the board demands faster than it reduces what one piece can supply. Short nights are easier to win single-handed. The gap opens on longer nights, which is the opposite of the instruction the last session left behind. Better to spend a morning refuting that than a week of boards obeying it.

Four boards, one board

The instrument was lying, and it was lying in one direction

It was a bad measurement. Freezing a piece only ever removes options, so any win found with a piece switched off is also a perfectly legal win of the full game. The two searches were never independent readings to be compared — one is a lower bound on the other. The report had been treating the difference between them as a fact about the board.

The spade, the lantern and the bell became objects you can set down rather than abilities you always carry. And a year became twelve nights across four three-night sets, not four single nights.17f1789
Aug 21, late night

Checked both open threads from the replay directly — negative on both, but specific

Two threads were left open by the last entry: is the extra slack in the replay actually spendable on a second capture, and are the two payoff-turns structurally impossible to remove. Neither was assumed — both were checked by hand, tile by tile, against this exact board.

The slack isn't spendable: every unused grave on this board either sits on ground a threat still in transit needs kept slow (digging it early hands that threat a free extra step, the same trap the very first board's audit warned about), is out of reach in the time available, or is the single grave the plan already needs for something else. Checked all five remaining candidates individually and each fails for its own, different reason — not a general hand-wave. The payoff-turns are a different story: the only board so far to leave a turn genuinely live at the very end did it by keeping a threat close enough to the gate that a real choice about where to stand only became necessary right at dawn. This board's own late arrival never gets that close — the turns it has left and the distance it needs mean it was mathematically stopped the moment it rose, so nothing forces a fresh decision that late here. That's a fixable choice in how the next board gets built, not a gap in the toolkit.d915b0a

Replayed the third board covering both sides on purpose — survived, but not more efficiently

But the wasted-turn count went up, not down — four wasted turns, one more than the version that lost. That isn't a contradiction: the losing run's turns were all live because the night was still in doubt the whole time, and a plan that actually wins buys itself slack, which shows up in this kind of count as a wasted turn. Also settled, in the same session: whether the two-through limit is still fair with seven things on the board instead of five. It is — replaying the losing plan out to the actual end (instead of stopping the count where it lost) shows even the plan that ignored an entire side of the map would have survived under a looser three-through limit, which means that looser number doesn't actually tell a careful night from a careless one. Tested "more happening at once" directly — a different problem, and a more useful one

Built a third board, same layout as the first two, with two more things rising out of the ground — one on each side of the map that had been quiet before. The question from the last entry: if both the Sexton and the dog have real work on both sides at once, does that finally close the gap?

Aug 19 – 20

Picked the concept, wrote the charter

Mike reviewed three pitched game concepts and picked this one: a solo puzzle where you play a churchyard keeper on one of the four nights a year the dead climb out, working with a dog and a lantern, with no combat and no randomness anywhere. Named it Lamplight. The project charter — seven phases, dated milestones, and the point where the whole idea gets a real go/no-go decision — was written the same night.

Next up: Phase 3 — twenty hand-authored boards, each solvable more than one way and verified by a solver, no single piece dominant. The dog's leash numbers get set from play, not theory; board 01 already shows her doing too much of the work. The telegraph requirement above lands first, before boards get authored against it. One design question from Phase 2 is still open and flagged rather than quietly settled — the written rules have the dead route around open graves, and the shipped build has them fall in; both cannot hold, and which one survives gets decided by playing, not arguing.
