What this is

A gravedigger, a dog, and no attack button

Lamplight is a small puzzle game. You play the Sexton — the churchyard keeper, on the hillside above town — on one of the four nights a year when what's buried gets up and starts walking. You have a dog, a lantern, and a handful of graves you can dig. You have eight turns before dawn.

There is no attack button. You can't hurt anything, and nothing can hurt you. The dead simply walk — by a fixed, printed rule, never a guess — toward the gate at the bottom of the hill, and if enough of them get through before sunrise, the town below loses. Your only tools are things a real churchyard sexton would actually have: you can dig a grave, push something into an open one and fill it back in, ring the bell to scatter whatever's nearby, and station your dog somewhere to make anything close by reluctant to walk past her. That's the whole toolkit. Everything else is routing a problem instead of removing it.

Nothing in this game is random, ever.

That's not flavor text. It's a hard rule the design isn't allowed to break: everything that happens on the board — where a corpse walks, how it reacts to the dog, what order things move in — is worked out by a fixed, printed formula, never a die roll. The lantern in your hand only lights the ground nearby; anything further off is following the exact same rules, just off-screen. A patient player with a pencil can work out, in advance, exactly what everything on the board will do all night, lit or not. If you get surprised, you did the math wrong — the game never rolled anything behind your back. That constraint is what makes this a real puzzle instead of a slot machine with sound effects, and it was Mike Carmody's condition at green-light, verbatim: fully predetermined, even when the player can't see it.

Phase 2 was the project's kill gate: at the end of it the central bet got tested for real. A board with no attack button either produces tension or it produces a chore, and the only honest way to find that out was for Mike to sit down with it on a phone and lose. If the answer had been "chore," the plan called for a pivot, a different concept, or a stop — instead two weeks bought a real answer instead of a hunch, and the answer was tension. No phase after this one could begin until that gate was explicitly passed; it now has been.

What Phase 1 could not settle, and what this build exists to settle, is whether there is enough to decide on each turn. Six sessions of solving boards by hand kept landing on three or four turns out of eight where the player had nothing worthwhile to do, against a self-imposed limit of two. The record of that is below, and the findings hold up — but hand-solving turned out to be the wrong instrument for the question, and the action economy was redesigned with Mike on August 21 rather than tuned further. The simulation now counts those dead turns itself, at runtime, every night anyone plays.

A wasted turn means exactly what it sounds like: the Sexton has nothing worthwhile to do and is just walking, or waiting, because the schedule leaves him no live choice. Every board hand-solved during Phase 1 landed at three or four such turns out of eight. That record is below, in the sessions that produced it.

Six sessions, six different findings

The first test board's core trick worked exactly as designed: corner a target in a dug grave, then bury it, on schedule. What didn't work was keeping the Sexton busy on the turns around it — his cycle is dig, walk, walk, spring the trap, fill it back in, and the walking turns are dead time by construction. The obvious fix was to make him faster. It made things worse: four wasted turns instead of three. The extra speed was meant to let him reach a second useful position late in the night, and he still arrived one tile short, every time — because that tile only opened up once a different creature vacated it on its own schedule. No amount of speed changes when someone else moves.

Two follow-up investigations then asked whether a second grave, placed differently, could pick up the slack. Both came back negative, for the same underlying reason: every grave on this board sits one tile in from the outer wall, and the creatures causing the trouble hug the wall itself — capturing them would mean standing off the map. That's a property of this one board's layout, not of the rules, so a second, purpose-built board moved a grave onto the true edge to test the fix directly. It worked — the capture came off clean. But the wasted-turn count didn't move. It's still four, and this time the cause is different: that board simply doesn't throw enough at the player, early enough, to keep both the Sexton and the dog occupied for a full night.

A third board tested that diagnosis directly by adding two more threats, one on each side of the map that had been quiet before. The wasted-turn count landed at three again — but this time not because nothing was possible. The night was lost because the whole session's attention went to one side of the map, leaving the other side to walk through completely unwatched. The decisions were there the entire night; they just weren't all taken.

The follow-up replayed that exact third board, splitting attention between both sides on purpose this time instead of letting one go unwatched. The night survived — the first time this design has held against seven threats at all, at the tighter pass-two limit and with nothing left over: two of the six stopped threats were still just one step from the gate at dawn. But the wasted-turn count went up, not down — four, one worse than the version that lost. That isn't a contradiction: the losing run's turns were all live because everything was still in doubt, and a plan that actually wins buys itself some slack, which is exactly what a wasted turn looks like in this kind of audit. Two of the four look like genuine unclaimed slack worth trying to close next; the other two are turns where an earlier decision is simply paying off, and those look hard to remove on a board shaped like this one at all.

A second question rode along with that replay: with seven threats instead of five, is the same pass-two-and-lose limit still fair, or should it loosen to three? Both got checked directly. The careful, both-flanks plan holds at the tighter number. So, it turns out, does the original losing plan — replayed out to the final turn instead of stopping the count where it lost historically, it never reaches the looser number either. That argues for keeping the tighter limit: one that saves the plan that ignored half the map just as readily as the one that didn't isn't measuring anything.

A sixth pass went back to that replay's own two open threads instead of building a new board. First: is the slack behind those four wasted turns actually free to spend on a second capture? Checked every remaining grave on the board against what's actually moving through it at the time, one by one, and each fails for its own reason — two sit on ground a threat still mid-route needs kept slow, one is the single grave the main plan already needs, and the rest are out of reach in the time available. Not unclaimed after all, though the check turned up a sharper, narrower version of the same question worth trying next: the collision that costs the main plan its capture traces back to exactly when the dog gets into position, which hasn't been tried different. Second: are the last two wasted turns a property of the rules, or of this one board? The first test board managed a genuinely live final turn once, by keeping a threat close enough to the gate that where to stand only became a real question at the very end. This board's own late arrival never gets that close — it was mathematically stopped the instant it rose — so nothing forces a fresh decision that late here. That's a board-building gap, not a rules one, and it says exactly what the next test board needs to do differently.

Those findings were not thrown away. Two of them are load-bearing and carry straight into the board authoring phase: two things through the gate is the right losing number, and dead turns late in a night are a property of how that particular night is scheduled rather than of the rules. What changed is who answers the question. It is no longer one person with a pencil solving one board at a time — it is the build, counting as it goes, and Mike, playing it.

Dev log

What happened, each session, in order

Aug 26

The lantern toggle was untestable, because nothing on screen was ever dark

Mike played the "5×5 / whole board" lantern-radius switch from RULES.md §7 — the open question of whether the withheld-information rule is worth its own confusion — and came back with a flatter problem than a design verdict: he couldn't tell the two settings apart. The sim has enforced the boundary correctly since Phase 2 (an unlit thing's path is withheld from the telegraph, its route unknown), but the board itself rendered every tile the same regardless of the setting. There was nothing to look at.

Fixed the actual gap: the sim's own lit/unlit check now draws the boundary it already enforces. Tiles outside the Sexton's Chebyshev radius darken — a heavy inset shadow and desaturation, not a subtle tint — so the two lantern settings are now visibly, immediately different, and the design question Phase 2 was supposed to answer by play can actually get answered by play. No rule changed; this was purely the renderer catching up to what the simulation already decided every turn.6415e2f
Aug 26 – 27

Two more boards into the solver, and the failure isn't one board's fault

Last session's solver had only ever measured one board, Michaelmas. Two more from Phase 1 existed as write-ups only — All Souls, built to test whether a grave on the true edge of a creature's own lane makes a capture actually work, and Candlemas, built to test whether two simultaneous flanks force real triage instead of one — but neither had ever been typed into the game's own board format, so the solver had never seen them. Ported both tonight, geometry and rising schedule only; the hand solves that went with them were played against a ruleset this project has since replaced, and re-deriving the answer from scratch is the entire point of having a solver.

Both fail the no-dominant-piece bar exactly like Michaelmas did. The gravedigger, the bell and the dog are each individually optional on every authored board in the project — three for three now. Candlemas is the sharper of the two results: it's the board that took three separate Phase 1 sessions just to survive, built specifically to force the player to cover two lanes at once, and the solver still finds a single-piece win on it. Whatever makes one piece dominate isn't a property of any one board's layout.
Every authored board so far uses only the Revenant — the default creature, the one with no resistance to any of the three pieces. The rules already define a second kind, the Curate, that ignores the dog outright, and nothing built yet has tried it. That's the more promising next lever, ahead of authoring more Revenant-only boards that this session's numbers suggest will land in the same place.893e60d
Aug 25 – 26

Built the solver. It failed every board in the project.

Phase 3's job is twenty hand-made boards, each one winnable more than one way, and none of them winnable while ignoring one of the three pieces. Both of those are claims that are very easy to make and very easy to be wrong about, so the phase starts with the machine that checks them rather than with the boards: a search that plays a night out against the real rules and reports the ways it found to survive. Twenty boards authored without one would be twenty boards verified by assertion.

It is deliberately a finder and not a prover, and the report says so on its own first page. Every plan it claims is replayed from the opening position through the ordinary game code and has to come back a win, so a reported solution cannot be an artefact of the search's own bookkeeping. The other direction does not hold: "no solution found" means this search did not find one, which is evidence and not proof. Overstating that would poison the entire phase, because every board decision after this point rests on it.54413fd
Then it was pointed at the boards and the news was bad. Michaelmas — the board solved by hand back in Phase 1 — turns out to be winnable three separate ways with a piece switched off: by the gravedigger alone, with the bell and the dog idle; by the dog alone, four moves and nothing else; and with the bell never rung. Six machine-generated boards were the same or worse, and one of them survives eight turns of the player doing literally nothing. Not one board in the project currently clears the bar. That was named as a risk in the original pitch — "the dog can trivialize the board" — and it has now arrived on schedule with numbers attached, which is the whole reason for measuring instead of asserting. It is a far sharper brief for the twenty boards than "escalating difficulty" was.
A second thing fell out on the way in, and it invalidated a number the project had been quoting. The scoring function that decides which of two positions is better had its sign the wrong way round: it rated a walking corpse standing at the churchyard gate as a better position than one stuck in the far corner. The automatic play-tester built on it had therefore been herding the dead toward the gate, and losing Michaelmas on turn six — a board known to be survivable. Five tests covered that function and not one went red, because every one of them checked the shape of the number and none asked which direction it pointed.
Aug 24 – 25

The churchyard now tells you what it is about to do

The first thing built after the kill gate, and it came straight out of Mike playing it: before you commit a turn, the board shows what every risen thing will do on the coming one — the tiles it steps onto, in order, and whether it ends up on the board, in an open grave, or through the gate. Into the Breach's contract. It takes nothing away, because nothing in this game was ever hidden behind a dice roll; the coming turn was always derivable with a pencil. This just does the arithmetic in front of you, and it recomputes after every single tap, so you watch your own move bend the prediction.

The design decision worth writing down: the prediction is produced by the resolver, not by a second copy of it. The turn resolver now records each step as it takes it, and the forecast is that same code run against a copy of the board. There is no parallel implementation that can drift, and a test plays ten generated boards to the end asserting the prediction and the turn never once disagree. In a game whose whole premise is perfect information, a telegraph that can lie would be worse than none at all.3c291a5
A rule had to be settled to ship it, and it was settled the honest way — by building both. The lantern is meant to show these routes only within two tiles of the Sexton, so that information is something you buy with position. That collides with showing everything. The rules document had already parked exactly this question on "we find out when it is playable," so the build carries a switch in the header that flips between the 5×5 lamplight and the whole board, and it can be answered by feel in one sitting. One consequence fell out of it: whether the turn loses the night is arithmetic too, so while anything is out in the dark the game says how many things it cannot preview rather than printing a verdict it is not entitled to.
Aug 24

Mike played it. The kill gate passed.

The whole reason Phase 2 exists: sit down with the build on a phone and find out for real whether a board you cannot attack is tension or a chore. Not simulated, not argued — played, twelve days ahead of the September 3 date the charter set aside for it.

Verdict: "the prototype seems good." Balance and timing still need work, but that was expected this early, not a mark against the core bet — his own words were "of course... but it's early." Phase 2 is closed. One piece of feedback carries straight into Phase 3 and is called out below rather than buried in a checklist: enemies and the environment should telegraph their next move before the player commits, Into the Breach style. It costs nothing new in the simulation — every board is already fully precomputable one step ahead, since nothing in this game hides information by design — it is exposing that in the interface, not changing the rules. It needs to land before boards get hand-authored, on the assumption that players can read a threat the way the Phase 1 hand-solver could.
Aug 22 – 23

Put it on a phone — the kill-gate artifact exists

A browser layer over the simulation, and then the part that actually matters: a public link. Tap a verb, tap a tile; the night runs through its phases; the schedule of everything that will rise is printed on the page, because nothing in this game is hidden. It is coloured squares with labels on them and it is meant to be — art is Phase 4.

Live at play.lamplight.mikecarmody.net. That link is Phase 2's whole deliverable — the go/no-go on this entire project is Mike opening it and losing on it.4318db4
Aug 22

Built the simulation — and it is deliberately boring code

Phase 1 closed. The first thing written was not the game: it was the automated build-and-test pipeline, because the machine this project is developed on is a Raspberry Pi that runs the household and is hard-blocked from compiling anything this size. Every check runs on GitHub's machines instead. That was a written prerequisite from day one rather than something discovered in week four.

Then the rules themselves, as plain functions with no graphics attached: board geometry, the distance-to-gate field, ground state and consecration, movement, the exact order in which the dead choose their next step, the dog's three rules, the turn pipeline, and seeded board generation that replays identically every time from the same number.

87 tests, green. The standing rule is that a rule with no test is not a real rule. The simulation also counts its own wasted turns as it runs — the measurement six sessions of pencil work were trying to take by hand is now free, on every night anyone plays, and it feeds a later phase directly.0ed7beb
Aug 21, late

Stopped hand-solving. Redesigned the action economy with Mike instead

Six sessions and roughly six hours had gone into solving boards by hand against a wasted-turn bar that Phase 1 never actually set as a pass/fail gate — it asked for a written answer to the question, and that answer had landed the day before. Mike's read was blunt and correct: this was not a viable way to design a game, and the instinct to keep trying until something proves out is the thing to stop doing. So the loop itself got redesigned, in conversation, in one sitting.

What changed: falling into an open grave is now automatic, which collapses the old five-turn removal cycle and turns digging into a prediction — you open the ground where you worked out something will be. Burial becomes optional, and a debt: everything climbs back out at dawn on its own, and burying costs a turn but keeps that one down for the rest of the year. The spade, the lantern and the bell became objects you can set down rather than abilities you always carry. And a year became twelve nights across four three-night sets, not four single nights.17f1789
Aug 21, late night

Checked both open threads from the replay directly — negative on both, but specific

Two threads were left open by the last entry: is the extra slack in the replay actually spendable on a second capture, and are the two payoff-turns structurally impossible to remove. Neither was assumed — both were checked by hand, tile by tile, against this exact board.

The slack isn't spendable: every unused grave on this board either sits on ground a threat still in transit needs kept slow (digging it early hands that threat a free extra step, the same trap the very first board's audit warned about), is out of reach in the time available, or is the single grave the plan already needs for something else. Checked all five remaining candidates individually and each fails for its own, different reason — not a general hand-wave. The payoff-turns are a different story: the only board so far to leave a turn genuinely live at the very end did it by keeping a threat close enough to the gate that a real choice about where to stand only became necessary right at dawn. This board's own late arrival never gets that close — the turns it has left and the distance it needs mean it was mathematically stopped the moment it rose, so nothing forces a fresh decision that late here. That's a fixable choice in how the next board gets built, not a gap in the toolkit.d915b0a
Aug 21, night

Replayed the third board covering both sides on purpose — survived, but not more efficiently

Same board as the last entry, same seven things rising, nothing moved. Only the plan changed: this time, split attention between both sides from the start instead of letting the dog commit fully west and never come back. The east-side threat that got through unopposed last time was the one specific thing this session set out to fix.

Survived — the first time this design has held a full night against seven things at once, at the tighter two-through limit, with nothing spare: two of the six stopped things were still just one step from the gate when dawn broke. But the wasted-turn count went up, not down — four wasted turns, one more than the version that lost. That isn't a contradiction: the losing run's turns were all live because the night was still in doubt the whole time, and a plan that actually wins buys itself slack, which shows up in this kind of count as a wasted turn. Also settled, in the same session: whether the two-through limit is still fair with seven things on the board instead of five. It is — replaying the losing plan out to the actual end (instead of stopping the count where it lost) shows even the plan that ignored an entire side of the map would have survived under a looser three-through limit, which means that looser number doesn't actually tell a careful night from a careless one. The tighter limit stays.ff0234e
Aug 21, night

Tested "more happening at once" directly — a different problem, and a more useful one

Built a third board, same layout as the first two, with two more things rising out of the ground — one on each side of the map that had been quiet before. The question from the last entry: if both the Sexton and the dog have real work on both sides at once, does that finally close the gap?

Result: the night was lost — two things got through, unopposed, on the side nobody was watching. But the reason is different and more useful than before: the player had real decisions almost the entire night, including a genuine two-move combination between the dog and the Sexton that showed up on its own rather than being planned — the dog knocked something off its path into a spot right next to the Sexton, who then shoved it straight back. The loss came from spending all of that attention on one side of the map while the other side walked through completely unchallenged. That's a harder, more interesting problem than "nothing to do": it's "can't watch both sides at once," and it's a fairer test of whether the design works. Also found and fixed a real gap in how the dog's own rule was written down — it read as "she distracts whoever is closest to the gate," which lets a fast, distant threat hog her attention for free, forever, even if she can never reach it. Fixed to "she only distracts something she can actually reach."954d8c1
Aug 21, afternoon

Built a second board to isolate the real problem

Built a brand-new test board, "All Souls," specifically to answer one question: does the push-into-a-grave trick actually work when the grave is positioned correctly — right on the edge of the map, directly in a target's path — instead of one tile off, like every grave on the first board happened to be?

Yes. Clean capture, worked exactly as intended. That confirms the core toolkit is sound. But the wasted-turn count still didn't improve — it's 4 on this board too, for a completely different reason: not enough things happen at once to keep both the Sexton and the dog busy for a full eight-turn night. That's a more useful failure than the first one, because it points at pacing, not the rules themselves, as the remaining problem.f4563ba
Aug 21, midday

Tried trapping instead of pushing — also dead, but the reason generalizes

Tested a different capture idea: instead of directly shoving something into an open grave, cut off every other direction it could walk so the grave is the only option left.

Doesn't work here either — and the reason turns out to apply to every grave on this entire board, not just the ones tried: it takes three blockers to fully cut off a target's escape routes, and there are only two mobile pieces to do it with. That's a real, reusable finding about this board's layout, even though the specific idea didn't pan out.2d8f6b1
Aug 21, late morning

Tried a second grave on the weak side — dead end

Different tactic, same board: use the Sexton's early free turns to dig a second grave near the side of the map giving the dog the most trouble, and use it to trap one of the two things coming from that direction.

Worked out the geometry by hand before committing to it, and it doesn't work on this board — the two graves near that side are positioned wrong relative to the path those things actually walk. Ruled out cleanly, without having to hand-play the whole night to find out.c3968c3
Aug 21, morning

Gave the Sexton more speed — made it worse

The obvious first fix: let the Sexton cover more ground each turn. Re-ran the exact same test board under the new rule to see if it helped.

Result: worse, not better — 4 wasted turns instead of 3. Traced exactly why: his one useful position was pinned by someone else's schedule, not his own speed, so moving faster didn't change when he could actually get there. Also found and closed a real gap in the rules along the way (what happens when a player and a walking corpse race for the same tile — it was never actually written down).a3b5eb2
Aug 20

Wrote the full rules, hand-solved the first board

Complete ruleset written down: turn order, how the dead move and decide where to go, how digging, burying and pushing work, win and loss conditions. Then hand-played the first test board ("Michaelmas") move by move on paper, writing down the reasoning behind every step — and it held. The night was survived.

But: the design's own bar for a good puzzle — no more than 2 turns out of 8 where the player has nothing real to decide — wasn't met. Michaelmas hit 3. Officially not closed.09309b7
Aug 19 – 20

Picked the concept, wrote the charter

Mike reviewed three pitched game concepts and picked this one: a solo puzzle where you play a churchyard keeper on one of the four nights a year the dead climb out, working with a dog and a lantern, with no combat and no randomness anywhere. Named it Lamplight. The project charter — seven phases, dated milestones, and the point where the whole idea gets a real go/no-go decision — was written the same night.

Next up: Phase 3 — twenty hand-authored boards, each solvable more than one way and verified by a solver, no single piece dominant. The dog's leash numbers get set from play, not theory; board 01 already shows her doing too much of the work. The telegraph requirement above lands first, before boards get authored against it. One design question from Phase 2 is still open and flagged rather than quietly settled — the written rules have the dead route around open graves, and the shipped build has them fall in; both cannot hold, and which one survives gets decided by playing, not arguing.
